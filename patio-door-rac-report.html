The finding that reframes everything

You are not choosing an installer. You are choosing who owns the parts supply for the next twenty years.

A sliding patio door is not a window. It has rollers, a multi-point latch, a handle set and — in the Lifestyle Series specifically — an integrated retractable screen with its own spring mechanism. All of those are wear parts, and on a proprietary branded system they are single-sourced through the dealer network. The most instructive evidence I found in this whole exercise was not a review score; it was a complaint pattern against a competing premium brand in this same market, describing a patio door whose locking mechanism failed after roughly four years and left the owner waiting about two and a half months for a proprietary part Unconfirmed — relayed by search, BBB profile not opened. The same body of complaints describes a defective patio slider replaced repeatedly with panels that were themselves defective, and a warranty threatened with voiding after the owner hired someone else to fix a leak.

That reframes the decision. The premium-brand question is not "is the door good" — it almost certainly is. It's "when a $40 roller fails in 2034, am I dependent on one regional dealer with a documented habit of not answering complaints?" That question ranks the field differently than any star rating does.

07 Law, warranty, risk

The policy layer

The governing law, and what you lose if it goes wrong

California's Home Improvement Contract rules sit in the Business & Professions Code and are enforced by the CSLB. Three provisions do almost all the protective work on a job this size:

RuleWhat it saysWhat you lose if it goes wrong
Written contract required Every home improvement job over $500 needs a written, legible, understandable contract; changes must also be written. Without it you are arguing about an oral scope. Verbal change orders are where "we didn't quote the stucco patch" lives.
Down payment cap The deposit may not exceed $1,000 or 10% of the contract price — whichever is less. No exception for special-order materials. This is your single biggest financial protection, and it is the one most often quietly broken on custom-order doors. On a $6,000 job your maximum lawful deposit is $600, not $1,000. A vendor asking for 50% up front on a special-order door is asking you to waive a statutory right.
Three-day right to cancel Unless the contract was negotiated at the contractor's own place of business, you get three days to cancel in writing under the Home Solicitation Sales Act, and the contractor must hand you the notice with the contract. Nearly every in-home sales visit triggers this. If you sign at your kitchen table and no cancellation notice is attached, the contract is defective from the start.

Third-party risk

  • Subcontracted labour. If crews are subcontracted, the sub's workers' comp — not the contractor's — may be what actually covers an injury on your property. Ask who employs the people in your house, and get the answer in writing.
  • Mechanics' liens. In California a supplier or subcontractor who isn't paid by your contractor can lien your house, even though you paid in full. Ask for unconditional lien releases from the contractor and any material supplier at each payment.
  • Manufacturer vs. installer split. The door is warranted by the factory; the installation by the contractor. Water intrusion sits exactly on that seam, and it is where both sides point at each other. Get one named party accountable for leaks in writing.
  • Permit in whose name. If you pull the permit yourself as owner-builder, you absorb liability the contractor would otherwise carry. Let the contractor pull it. San José Development Services: 408-535-3555.

Your contract protections — the checklist

  1. Deposit capped at the lesser of $1,000 or 10%. Write the number in yourself before signing.
  2. Three-day cancellation notice physically attached if you sign anywhere but their office.
  3. CSLB licence number, entity legal name and expiry printed on the contract — then check it at cslb.ca.gov yourself.
  4. Certificate of insurance sent directly by their broker to your email — never a PDF forwarded by the salesperson. General liability and workers' comp, with you listed as certificate holder.
  5. Payments tied to completed milestones, never to calendar dates. Final payment after your inspection, not after theirs.
  6. Flashing and waterproofing method described in the written scope, not assumed.
  7. Parts-availability commitment in writing — see §10 for the exact wording.
  8. Unconditional lien release at each payment.

09 In plain English

My assessment, in plain English

The situation, simply

You saw a nice patio door in a video. It's a Pella. You want one, or something like it, in your house in San Jose. There are about five outfits who could do this job, and they fall into two camps: local family firms who will fit whatever door you buy, and brand operations who will only fit their own door.

I was asked to check them properly. I mostly couldn't. The state licence website — the one that says whether a contractor is real, insured and in trouble — is blocked from this computer. So is Yelp. So is the Better Business Bureau. So is Pella's own site. I've been straight about that everywhere below, and I've given you the phone numbers to finish the job in about twenty minutes.

Why your priority beats price

You didn't send me a budget. You sent me a door. So I've assumed you care more about getting the right door, fitted properly, than about saving four hundred dollars.

Here's why that's the right way round anyway. A patio door is the largest moving object in your house. It has wheels, a lock and a screen, and all of those wear out. The cheapest quote saves you money once. The wrong choice of who supplies your parts costs you every time something breaks for the next twenty years. I found a case in this market where a lock failed and the owner waited about two and a half months for the part, because only one company could sell it to them. That's the risk that actually matters, and it isn't on any price list.

The recommendation

Start with Almaden Window & Door.

The headline reason: they're in San Jose, they're independent, and they aren't tied to one factory — so when something breaks in ten years, you have options and they're ten minutes away.

Then, in order:

  1. They can fit a Pella if you want one, or something better-value if you don't. The brand operations can only sell you their own door. Almaden can sell you the right one.
  2. A local firm has to live with its reputation. They're a few miles from you and their customers are your neighbours. A regional franchise dispatching from Hayward doesn't carry that pressure.
  3. Nothing bad turned up. No complaint pattern, no adverse record, no unanswered grievances. That's a weaker statement than it sounds — I couldn't open the registry — but it's better than what I found on two of the others.

The honest catch: there's much less evidence about them than about Brothers — 78 reviews against 512 — and their licence number looks about three years old while they claim to have been trading since 1995. That's probably a company re-registration and completely innocent. But you should ask, because the guarantee behind your contract belongs to the company, not the family.

The runner-up

Brothers Home Improvement. They have the best evidence of anyone here by a distance — an independent consumer group surveyed 76 of their customers and 86% rated them superior, at prices about 1% below average, and their licence number genuinely does date from 1994 like they say. If Almaden's answer about the licence is unconvincing, go here without hesitating. The only catch is that they mainly do vinyl doors, which isn't the wood-and-glass door in your video. If you're relaxed about that, they might honestly be the better buy.

Who you didn't pick, and why

  • Renewal by Andersen — a large, competent, well-run operation, and their installers get real praise. But the complaints I found were about patio doors specifically: defective panels, a long wait for a lock part, a leak dispute. Wrong shape of risk for you. A different buyer could use them happily.
  • Pella direct — the only people who can sell you the exact door from the video. Held back because the local Pella company scores B− with the BBB for not replying to two complaints, and there's no San Jose showroom. If the retracting screen is a must-have, go here anyway and use the contract protections in §07.
  • Blossom Hill Glass — not really a competitor. Keep their number in case you decide to upgrade the glass instead of replacing the whole door.
  • Home Depot and Lowe's — they subcontract the actual work, so you can't check who's turning up. That's the one thing I'd tell you never to accept on this job.

The uncomfortable part you should know

This report is thinner than it looks. It has the shape of a verified assessment, but I could not open a single primary record — not one licence, not one review, not one warranty document. Every fact here reached me second-hand through a search engine's summary of pages I never saw.

I've marked all of that honestly, and the ranking is built on structural reasoning that doesn't depend on the missing data — locality, brand independence, parts risk — which is why I'll still stand behind it. But if you take one thing from this: spend twenty minutes at cslb.ca.gov and on the phone before you sign anything. That check is worth more than everything above it.

And a fairness note on the other direction: I found nothing bad about Almaden or Blossom Hill, but a failed or impossible search is not evidence of a clean record. It is just an absence of evidence. I have not cleared anyone.
