The finding that reframes everything

You've been sold on "licensed & insured." For a weekly mow, half of that is legally irrelevant.

In California, routine lawn mowing, edging, weeding and blowing is not "contracting" at all — the Business & Professions Code explicitly carves gardeners out of the CSLB licence system, and even licensable landscape construction only needs a licence once a single job hits $1,000 (raised from $500 on 1 Jan 2025). So a vendor waving a "licensed" banner for a $55 mow is answering a question you didn't ask.

The real exposure is money and bodies: if a worker with no insurance is injured on your property, you can be treated as their employer and the bill lands on your homeowner's policy — or on you. That flips the whole screen. The filter isn't "who's licensed." It's "whose liability coverage and recourse can I put in writing before they set foot on my lawn."

The rules that govern this

The policy layer

The governing law, and what you lose if it goes wrong

The single most important legal fact, and the one that reframes the whole decision: routine lawn mowing is not "contracting" in California and needs no contractor licence. The gardener carve-out is written into the definition of "contractor" itself, and even licensable landscape construction only trips the licence requirement at $1,000 per job (raised from $500 on 1 Jan 2025). Correction to a common belief the "$500 rule" you may have heard is outdated.

Source: CA Business & Professions Code §7026.1 (gardener carve-out), §7048 (minor-work exemption); AB 2622 (2024), eff. 1 Jan 2025 · leginfo.legislature.ca.gov; CSLB "AB 2622 Implementation" bulletin · cslb.ca.gov. (The enacted California statutes and the state licensing board's own implementation guidance — the controlling authorities on when a licence is required. Read via web search 11 Aug 2026; not directly openable — confirm the $1,000 figure on the leginfo §7048 page.)

What you lose if you get the licence question wrong (relevant only if you add construction/hardscape ≥$1,000, not for a mow): if you hire an unlicensed person for licensable work and they're injured on your property, you can be deemed their employer — the bill for injury and rehab lands on your homeowner's policy or on you personally, with no bond and no CSLB complaint process to fall back on. Unlicensed contracting is itself a misdemeanour.

Source: CA Labor Code §2750.5; CSLB "The Downside of Being an Owner-Builder" · cslb.ca.gov. (§2750.5 is the enacted statute recharacterising unlicensed workers as employees; the CSLB page is the board's official consumer warning. Read via web search 11 Aug 2026; not directly openable.)

Third-party risk & your insurance exposure

On every marketplace here the worker is an independent contractor, not the platform's employee — so if things go badly the platform's guarantee is your first recourse, not an employer's liability. Your own exposure is governed by California's residential-employee rules: a gardener working outside your home ≥10 hrs/week is treated as a full-time employee (Labor Code §3351(d)); below the casual-worker thresholds — <52 hours in the prior 90 days or <$100 earned — there's no statutory workers'-comp claim, but the worker can still sue you in ordinary negligence, where there's no cap. Since 1977, California homeowner policies are deemed to include workers'-comp for qualifying residential employees (Insurance Code §11590) — but short-duration and casual workers typically fall outside it, leaving you personally exposed. This is the whole reason the reframing finding points at insurance, not licences.

Source: CA Labor Code §3351(d), §3352(h); Insurance Code §11590 · leginfo.legislature.ca.gov; CA DIR/DWC · dir.ca.gov. (Enacted statutes defining residential-employee status, the casual-worker exclusion, and the imputed homeowner-policy workers'-comp coverage; DIR administers workers' comp. Gig-worker classification (AB 5) & warranty terms

Under AB 5 and the ABC test (Labor Code §2775; Dynamex, 2018), a worker is presumed an employee unless the hirer proves all three prongs. Hiring an established, business-licensed, insured operator keeps employer status on the vendor; directing a lone individual with your equipment on your schedule can flip it onto you. On warranties: the only genuine written warranty in the set is a service guarantee (re-mow/refund) — none is a manufacturer-style product warranty, and none of the money-back promises is a substitute for the vendor's own liability insurance.

Source: CA Labor Code §§2775–2787; Dynamex Operations West v. Superior Court (2018) 4 Cal.5th 903; CA DIR/DLSE independent-contractor FAQ · dir.ca.gov. (The enacted AB 5 statutes, the controlling California Supreme Court decision, and the labour agency's official guidance. Read via web search 11 Aug 2026; not directly openable.)

Your contract protections

For anything solicited or signed at your door for $25+, California gives you a 3-day right to cancel (5 days if you're 65+; 7 for disaster repair) under Civil Code §1689.6, and the seller must hand you the written cancellation notice (§1689.7). Note: a service you initiate by calling a listed business may fall outside "home solicitation." Insist on: a written scope + flat price + schedule; a certificate of insurance sent by the vendor's broker, not a photo of a card; the guarantee in writing; and payment by a traceable method. Keep any deposit small — the CSLB benchmark for licensed home-improvement work caps it at $1,000 or 10%.

Source: CA Civil Code §1689.6 & §1689.7 · leginfo.legislature.ca.gov; CA DCA / CSLB consumer guidance. (The enacted home-solicitation cancellation statutes and the state consumer-affairs agencies' guidance. Read via web search 11 Aug 2026; not directly openable.)

No jargon

My assessment, in plain English

The situation, simply

You need the grass cut every week or two. You can hire a neighbourhood gardener for cash, book a gig worker by the hour, or use a service that sends someone and stands behind the job. They cost roughly the same per month. The difference isn't the mowing — it's what happens on a bad day.

Why your priority beats price

The cheapest option saves you maybe $30–$50 a month. But if an uninsured worker trips on your step, or a mower flings a rock through a window, the person legally on the hook can be you — and that bill is measured in thousands, not dollars. Paying a little more for someone who carries insurance and stands behind the work isn't overpaying. It's buying out the one risk that can actually hurt you. That's why "covered and reliable" beats "cheapest."

The recommendation

Go with LawnStarter. One headline reason: it's the only option that puts real damage protection — $2 million — and documented background checks in writing, which is exactly the risk that matters here.

Three more, ranked:

  1. It's genuinely hands-off — set price, automatic weekly scheduling, no managing anyone.
  2. It has a real guarantee — a bad mow gets redone or refunded within five days.
  3. It's the cheapest per visit in the managed tier, so you're not paying a premium for the protection.

The honest catch: its complaint record is about cancellations and slow support. So you might occasionally have to chase a rescheduled visit. That's an annoyance, not a financial risk — and it's the trade I'd make.

The runner-up

Lawn Love — same hands-off model, the only one BBB-accredited, with a clean money-back guarantee. The only reason it's second is that it doesn't publish a damage-cover number, and that number is the whole point here.

Who you didn't pick, and why

GreenPal — clever "approve before you pay" protection and consistent ratings, but no background check and no damage cover. A local gardener — probably the best value on the street, but I couldn't verify a single one this session, and the cheap cash operator is the most likely to carry no insurance. TaskRabbit — you asked about it; it's built for one-off jobs and openly says it isn't an insurer, so it's the wrong tool for a standing weekly mow (fine for a one-time cleanup).
