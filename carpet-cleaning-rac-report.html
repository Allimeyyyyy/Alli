◆ The finding that reframes everything

"Licensed and insured" — the phrase every one of these companies uses — is close to meaningless in this trade, and the insurance half is the dangerous half.

The licence half. There is no California state licence for carpet cleaning. The Contractors State License Board licenses construction; its flooring class, C-15, covers preparing surfaces and installing carpet, not cleaning it. Cleaning that doesn't alter the structure falls outside the licensing scheme entirely. So "licensed" here means a City of San José business tax certificate — a registration anyone can obtain by paying a tax. There is no exam, no bond, no disciplinary file, and no complaint history for you to search. The consumer-protection apparatus you instinctively assume sits behind the word "licensed" does not exist for this purchase.

Source: CSLB — Licensing Classifications, C-15 Flooring & Floor Covering · cslb.ca.gov/about_us/library/licensing_classifications/ (California Contractors State License Board — the state agency that issues, renews and disciplines contractor licences and defines every trade classification; the authoritative record for what is and isn't a licensed trade. Read 17 Aug 2026 via search; direct page load blocked.)
Source: City of San José — Business Tax & Registration, SJMC §4.76 · sanjoseca.gov/your-government/departments-offices/finance/business-tax-registration (The city Finance Department page setting the registration duty; San José Municipal Code requires every business operating in the city — including home-based and mobile ones — to register within 90 days. This is the only "licence" a carpet cleaner here actually needs. Read 17 Aug 2026.)

The insurance half — this is the part that can cost you money. A standard commercial general liability policy contains a care, custody and control exclusion: it does not pay for damage to the customer's property that was in the contractor's care while they worked on it. Read that again against this job. The thing in their care is your carpet, your sofa, your wool rug, your hardwood at the room transitions. A cleaner can hold a perfectly valid GL policy, say "fully insured" with a straight face, and still have no coverage for the only loss this job realistically produces. Cover for that has to be bought separately, as a care/custody/control endorsement or an equivalent bailee form.

Source: Moody Insurance — "Understanding Care, Custody & Control Coverage for Cleaning Businesses" · moodycleaninsurance.com (A specialist insurance brokerage writing for cleaning contractors — an industry practitioner source, not an independent test, so treat as directional on market practice; the underlying CCC exclusion in the standard ISO general liability form is not in dispute. Corroborated by Insureon's cleaning-business GL guidance. Read 17 Aug 2026.)

So the whole evaluation inverts. You cannot verify a carpet cleaner the way you verify a contractor, because the state record you'd check doesn't exist. Private substitutes have to carry the load instead: a broker-issued certificate of insurance with the right endorsement, IICRC Certified Firm status, an audited accreditation such as Diamond Certified, and a written guarantee with a real deadline on it. Stars are the weakest of these, and stars are the only thing all five candidates have.

Third-party risk

  • Subcontracting. Nothing published tells you whether any of these firms subcontracts overflow work at peak. If they do, the COI you were shown may not cover the person who actually arrives. Ask: "will the technician be your W-2 employee or the owner, and does your policy cover subcontractors?"

  • Lead-generation marketplaces. If you book via HomeAdvisor/Angi or Thumbtack rather than directly, your contract is with the pro but your details are sold as a lead. Expect follow-up calls from competitors. Booking direct avoids this entirely.

Your contract protections — the terms to insist on

None of this needs a lawyer. Get it in an email before the visit and you have a written contract.

  • Itemised written quote naming rooms, stair count, each upholstery piece, and every treatment — with the total. This alone kills the doorstep upsell.
  • Certificate of insurance sent directly by their broker, showing general liability limits and care/custody/control cover. Broker-issued, not vendor-forwarded — a forwarded PDF proves nothing.
  • Guarantee with a number in it: "if spots reappear or you are not satisfied within 30 days, we re-clean at no charge." A window and a remedy, in writing.
  • Wastewater clause: "all wastewater will be discharged to the sanitary sewer, not the storm drain, in accordance with San José Municipal Code Title 15." One sentence moves that liability off your address.
  • No price change on arrival without your written agreement — and no work starts until you've agreed any variation.
  • Pay by credit card. Not Zelle, not cash, not Venmo. A card gives you chargeback rights, which — given there is no licensing board — is realistically your strongest single remedy.

09 Assessment

My assessment, in plain English

No jargon. Short sentences.

The situation, simply

You want your carpets, sofa and tile cleaned. You found a company you liked the look of. You want to know if they're any good, and who else you should be talking to.

Here is the thing nobody tells you. Anyone can clean carpets for money in California. There is no state licence for it. No test. No board that can discipline them. When a carpet cleaner says "licensed", they mean they paid the city a business tax. That's all it means.

And "insured" is worse. Most standard business insurance does not cover damage to the thing you were hired to work on. So a cleaner can be genuinely insured, and still not be covered if they ruin your carpet. That cover is a separate add-on. Most people never ask for it.

Why your priority beats price

You didn't ask me for the cheapest. You pointed at a specific company's service page. So I read you as wanting the job done properly.

That's the right instinct here, and the numbers back it. The whole spread between the cheapest and the dearest option is maybe two hundred dollars. Replacing carpet in three bedrooms is several thousand. Getting this wrong costs ten times what choosing well costs. Saving eighty dollars is not worth that risk.

There's a second reason. A bad clean is not neutral — it's worse than no clean. Over-wetting leaves carpet damp under the backing for days. That's how you get mildew and a smell that never quite leaves. Cheap operators work fast, and working fast is exactly how over-wetting happens.

The recommendation

Book Sapphire Steamer. Ask for proof of insurance first.

The headline reason: the owner does the work himself. So the reviews are about him, not about a company's hiring policy. You know exactly who is coming.

Then, in order:

  • He does all four things you need — carpet, upholstery, leather, tile. Leather is the fussy one and most competitors don't list it at all.
  • Two separate platforms, both perfect. 5.0 on Yelp across 75 reviews, 5.0 on Google across 107. Not one place — two, independently.
  • He's the only one who mentions wastewater disposal. Small thing. But dumping it down a storm drain is illegal in San José, and it's your driveway. Someone who thought about that has thought about other things too.

The honest catch: he's one man with one van. If he's sick, you wait. A bigger company would just send someone else. And 75 reviews is a smallish sample — a perfect score there is less impressive than a 4.9 across 299.

The runner-up

Dalux. 4.9 stars across 299 reviews, plus a 5.0 on HomeAdvisor. That's the biggest pile of evidence on this page, and holding 4.9 that long is genuinely hard. They also do same-day. If Sapphire can't produce an insurance certificate, or can't fit you in, call Dalux and don't feel you're settling.
