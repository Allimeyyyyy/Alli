◆ The finding that reframes everything

"It's low voltage, so it doesn't need a permit or an electrician" is wrong for the system you'd actually buy.

The exemption everyone in this trade leans on has two conditions, not one. San José exempts electrical work "operating at less than 25 volts and not capable of supplying more than 50 watts of energy." A 12-volt garden system clears the first condition easily. It fails the second one immediately: the transformer that runs even a modest scheme is typically 150 W to 600 W. Published transformer prices of $150–$800 describe exactly that class of hardware.

So the honest picture is a split job. The 12 V side — fixtures, cable runs, aiming, the part you can actually see — is landscaping craft. The 120 V side — the outdoor GFCI receptacle, the circuit feeding it, the transformer connection — is permitted electrical work that belongs to a C-10 licence holder. Most of the marketing in this category quietly collapses those two halves into one, and that is where homeowners get hurt: not by bad fixtures, but by an unpermitted, unlicensed 120 V connection buried behind a nice-looking garden.

What this changes: you are not shopping for "a garden light installer." You are commissioning a design-led 12 V install and a small, permitted 120 V electrical job — and your job is to make sure one contract covers both, with a named licence holder against each half.

The policy layer

The law that governs this job, what it costs you if it goes wrong, and the clauses that protect you.

The governing law — and a contradiction I could not resolve

SB 216 (2022) extended California's mandatory workers' compensation requirement to contractor classifications that were previously allowed a no-employee exemption, phasing in by classification and reaching all licensees on 1 January 2026. Then SB 1455 (2024) — the CSLB sunset-extension bill — pushed full implementation to 1 January 2028, reinstated the exemption, and directed CSLB to build a process for verifying exemption eligibility by 1 January 2027.

⚠ Sources disagree — do not act on a date from this report

Several insurance-industry sources still publish the pre-SB 1455 timeline, including a hard "file proof of workers' comp with CSLB by 1 July 2026" deadline. Others describe that deadline as superseded. I could not open the statutory text to settle it. The practical consequence for you is the same either way: ignore the date entirely and read the Workers' Compensation block on each contractor's CSLB License Detail page. That page shows what is actually on file today, which is the only fact that protects you.

What you lose if the licence question goes wrong

This is the part most homeowners don't know, and it is genuinely asymmetric — the downside lands on you, not the contractor:

  • You may become the employer. Under Insurance Code §2750.5, someone performing work that requires a licence, without holding one, is not an independent contractor. Hire an unlicensed contractor and that person — and potentially their crew — can be treated as your employee. If one of them falls off a ladder in your garden, you can be liable for the medical costs, and your homeowner's policy may decline it.
  • Your recourse runs the other way too. Business & Professions Code §7031 lets you sue to recover all compensation paid to an unlicensed contractor — even if the work was done perfectly. That's a real remedy, but it's a lawsuit, not a warranty, and it is a poor substitute for hiring correctly the first time.
  • Unpermitted electrical work follows the house. A 120 V connection made without a permit doesn't fail immediately — it surfaces at resale, at an insurance claim, or when the next owner's inspector opens the transformer enclosure.

Third-party risk

  • The subcontractor you didn't choose. If Pearman or R & J brings in an electrician for the 120 V work, that person's licence, insurance and workers' comp are what actually protect you — not the name on your contract. Name them in the document.
  • The manufacturer is not your counterparty. Fixture warranties run from the manufacturer to the purchaser. If the contractor bought the fixtures, they may be the purchaser of record, not you. Ask in writing who holds the warranty and get the paperwork transferred at handover.
  • Labour is never covered by the fixture warranty. A lifetime brass warranty replaces the casting. It does not pay anyone to dig it up, swap it and re-aim it. That gap is the contractor's workmanship warranty, and it's the one nobody here publishes.
  • Your HOA. 95138 contains HOA-governed communities. Exterior lighting can require architectural approval. Ask — check before you commission a design, not after.

Warranty terms — what "lifetime" actually means

The professional fixture tier does carry serious warranties, and this is a real reason to pay for it. VOLT publishes a lifetime warranty on brass and copper fixtures, LED bulbs and low-voltage transformers, with two-way shipping. AMP Lighting applies a lifetime warranty to brass, copper and stainless fixtures, LED bulbs and transformer cores and housings. Alliance Outdoor Lighting covers brass, copper and bronze castings and housings for life. FX Luminaire — the line Pearman names — sits in the same brass-forward professional tier.

Read the boundary carefully: these warranties cover the casting, the housing, the bulb. They are not a system warranty. Corroded connectors, nicked cable, a transformer sized wrong for the load, and fixtures aimed into your neighbour's bedroom are all workmanship — and all outside every one of these documents.

Your contract protections — the seven clauses

1 · The deposit cap is law, not etiquette

A home-improvement down payment may not exceed $1,000 or 10% of the contract price, whichever is less, excluding finance charges. A contractor asking for "half up front" is asking you to help them break B&P §7159. On a $5,000 job your legal maximum deposit is $500.

2 · Your right to cancel

Home-improvement contracts carry a written notice of the right to cancel — three business days, extended to five for buyers aged 65 or over on contracts signed since 1 January 2021. If the notice isn't in the document, the document isn't compliant.

3 · Name the C-10 against the 120 V scope

One sentence: "All 120 V work will be performed by [name], CSLB licence #[number], under permit from the City of San José." This is the clause that turns §2's rule into something enforceable.

4 · Permit responsibility, in writing

State who pulls the permit and that final payment is contingent on a passed inspection. Never accept "you don't need a permit for low voltage" as an answer — see §1 on the 50-watt clause.

5 · The insurance certificate comes from the broker

Not a PDF the contractor emails you. A certificate sent directly by their insurance broker, naming general liability and workers' compensation, with you listed as certificate holder so you're notified if it lapses.

6 · Workmanship warranty, separately stated

The fixture warranty is the manufacturer's. Get the labour warranty in writing — how long, what it covers, and specifically whether re-aiming after the first growing season is included. Two years is a reasonable ask.

7 · Hold a retention until dark

Withhold a meaningful final payment until you have walked the garden at night and signed off on aiming and glare. Lighting is the one trade where daytime acceptance is meaningless.

Bonus · Ask for the as-built

A simple plan showing cable runs, splice locations, transformer taps and wattage per zone. In five years, when you plant a tree, this document is worth more than the warranty.

09 Assessment

My assessment, in plain English

No jargon. Short sentences.

The situation, simply

You want your garden lit. That sounds like one job. It is actually two.

The first job is the pretty part. Where the lights go. Which tree gets lit from below. How bright. That is a design skill. Landscapers have it. Electricians usually don't.

The second job is a small piece of electrical work. The lights run on 12 volts, but the box that makes 12 volts has to be plugged into the house at 120 volts. That connection needs a licensed electrician and, in San Jose, almost certainly a permit.

Nearly everyone selling to you will tell you the second job doesn't exist. It does.

Why your priority beats price

You didn't tell me a priority, so I picked one for you: get it right, not cheap. Here is why.

Cheap garden lighting fails in a specific, predictable way. The fixtures are aluminium. They corrode. Within a couple of seasons half of them are dark. Then you pay again.

Good fixtures are brass. They last decades and the makers warrant them for life. The difference in price is maybe two thousand dollars. The difference in outcome is doing this once instead of three times.

And the legal side is worse than the money side. If you hire someone unlicensed and their worker gets hurt in your garden, California can treat that worker as your employee. Your home insurance may not cover it. That risk is not worth saving a few hundred dollars on a quote.

◆ Final verdict

Pearman Landscape Management, Inc.

Call Pearman Landscape Management on Monday, send them the email in §10, and tell them the quote will not be considered unless it names the C-10 electrician who will do the 120 V work.

Why I chose them

Because you asked for a garden light installer, and Pearman is the only candidate in this field where lighting reads as a craft someone has practised, rather than a page someone commissioned. That distinction is the whole call.

The evidence that moved me, weighted honestly. Heaviest: the fixture line. Pearman is the only vendor of eight who names a professional brass-forward manufacturer. That single fact tells me more about what will be standing in your garden in 2036 than every review in this report combined, because the failure mode in this category is corrosion, not craftsmanship. Second: proximity. Twelve miles down 101 versus eighteen across the valley sounds trivial and isn't — garden lighting needs re-aiming after the first growing season, and the contractor who comes back is the one for whom coming back is cheap. Third, and lighter than it looks: the 4.7 from 41 reviews. It's the best rating in the set on the platform that actually covers this trade, but almost all of it is maintenance work, so I've treated it as evidence they treat customers well, not as evidence they light gardens well.

What I deliberately gave little weight to: review volume. Tested and True has roughly twelve times Pearman's footprint. It is almost entirely panel upgrades and EV chargers. A large public record of a different job is not evidence about your job, and I'd rather say that plainly than let a big number do the deciding.
