The single rule that eliminates candidates

The elimination rule
If the exact configuration you are ordering cannot show a U-factor of 0.30 or lower and an SHGC of 0.23 or lower on its NFRC label, it is out — no matter how good the door, the brand or the price. These are legal maximums for Climate Zone 4, not targets. A door rated 0.31 does not pass inspection.
Source: California Energy Commission — Title 24 Building Energy Efficiency Standards, 2025 cycle (in effect for 2026) · energy.ca.gov Not verified — could not open
(The California Energy Commission is the state agency that writes and enforces the Title 24 energy code — the authoritative source for fenestration U-factor and SHGC limits by climate zone. San Jose sits in Climate Zone 4. The 0.30 / 0.23 ceiling and the "maximums, not targets" framing were relayed by search summary and by Milgard's own Title 24 guidance; the Commission's own code text was not opened, so confirm before ordering. Relayed 17 Aug 2026.)

Why this rule does the real work. It is objective, it is checkable on a sticker, and it is the only criterion in this report that can void a purchase outright. It also cuts across brand: a Pella, a Milgard and a JELD-WEN can each pass or fail depending on the glass package you tick. That is why every email in §10 asks for the NFRC number in writing before money moves — the rating belongs to the configuration, not to the brand on the box.

One honest caveat on the rule: the 0.30/0.23 pair is the widely-cited prescriptive limit, and Title 24 also permits a whole-building performance path where a single non-compliant component can be offset elsewhere. For a one-door replacement the prescriptive path is what you will be held to. Ask your permit desk — San José Development Services, 408-535-3555.

Third-party risk

  • Retailer vs manufacturer. Buying through Lowe's or Home Depot means the retailer handles the transaction but the manufacturer owns the warranty. Two companies, one door, and a seam between them. Establish before buying which one you call when the roller fails.
  • Installation voids things. Manufacturer warranties are routinely conditional on correct installation. If you buy the unit yourself and hire an installer separately, a leak dispute has both parties pointing at each other. Buying supply-and-install from one accountable party removes that seam.
  • Freight damage. An 80-inch glass unit ships by freight. Inspect before signing the delivery note — a signature usually closes your claim.
  • Discontinuation. The longest-term risk in a proprietary system. A lifetime warranty on a discontinued series is worth whatever the maker still stocks.

Your protections — the checklist

  1. NFRC-certified U-factor and SHGC for your exact configuration, in writing, before ordering. The number belongs to the config, not the brand.
  2. Full warranty PDF — not a marketing summary — read before purchase, checking specifically what voids it.
  3. Written confirmation of whether Rolscreen (or any retractable screen) is included in the SKU you are buying.
  4. Return and restocking terms for a special-order door, in writing. Special orders are frequently non-returnable — this is where the Title 24 risk becomes financial.

09 In plain English

My assessment, in plain English

The situation, simply

You saw a patio door in a video and you want to buy one. It's a Pella. There are five or six doors you could realistically order, and they split into two groups: vinyl doors that are cheaper, well warranted and completely ordinary to look at, and wood doors that cost more and look like the video.

There's a catch nobody mentions. The clever bit in that video — the screen that rolls away into the frame — is an optional extra. The versions of that door I can find for sale online don't say they include it. So you might buy the right door and get the wrong screen.

There's also a rule you can't get around. San Jose is in Climate Zone 4, and California law caps how much heat a new door can let through. If the glass you order misses that number, it fails inspection after it's installed. That's the expensive way to find out.

And I have to tell you plainly: I couldn't see a single price. Every shop and manufacturer site was blocked from this machine. I have real product links and real part numbers, but no prices at all.

Why your priority beats price

You've now sent me that video twice. Nobody does that about a door they're lukewarm on. So I've assumed you want that door — the wood inside, the screen that vanishes — rather than the cheapest thing that slides.

That's the right way round, for one unglamorous reason. This is the biggest moving object in your house and you'll touch it every day for twenty years. The gap between the cheap door and the nice door is probably a four-figure sum once. The gap between liking it and tolerating it is every single day. Price matters least on the things you use most.

The recommendation

Buy the Pella Lifestyle Series — but confirm the screen before you pay.

The headline reason: it's the only door here that's actually the one you asked about. Nothing else combines a wood interior with a screen that disappears.

Then, in order:
