Working assumption

The single rule that eliminates candidates

If the LED is sealed into the fixture, the fixture is a consumable — and it's out.

Every long-warranty landscape fixture on the market fails the same way: not the brass, not the lens, but the light engine and its driver. In a lamp-ready fixture — a socket that takes a standard G4 bi-pin or MR16 bulb — that failure costs you a few dollars and ninety seconds with the shade off. In an integrated-LED fixture, the same failure means digging up the stake and replacing the whole unit, and any warranty on it is a warranty on your paperwork, not on your evening. Lamp-ready solid brass, or it does not make the matrix. This rule alone eliminates Philips Hue Calla, Hampton Bay, every solar stake light, and most of Kichler's integrated Design Pro range.

Section 08

The policy layer

The governing law, and what you lose if it goes wrong

California Energy Code, Title 24 Part 6, §150.0(k) — residential lighting. All lighting attached to the residence or to other buildings on the same lot must be high-efficacy, controlled by a manual on/off switch plus one automatic control type: a photocontrol combined with either a motion sensor or an automatic time-switch. Any manual override to ON must return to automatic operation within six hours. The 2025 code edition is the current one.

What it means for you: a wall lantern bolted to your house is squarely covered. Six freestanding brass path lights staked in a bed, fed from a plug-in transformer, are the classic grey zone — they are not attached to a building, and low-voltage landscape work generally does not trigger a permit that would bring Title 24 compliance into scope. Unconfirmed for San Jose specifically. What you lose if it goes wrong: realistically, a correction notice at a future permit inspection, not a fine today. And the practical mitigation is free — the recommended kit ships with a transformer whose timer and photocell already satisfy the control requirement, so buying compliant costs you nothing.

California Energy Commission — 2025 Single-Family Residential Compliance Manual, Chapter 6 (Lighting) · energy.ca.gov(The California Energy Commission is the state agency that writes and enforces the Title 24 Part 6 energy code; its compliance manual is the authoritative interpretation of §150.0(k). Tier-one government source. Read 17 Aug 2026 via search extract.)

NEC Article 411 — low-voltage lighting systems. Landscape lighting operating at 30V or less falls under Article 411, and systems at 50V or less are generally exempt from permit requirements in most jurisdictions. Separately, NEC 210.8 requires GFCI protection on all 15- and 20-amp, 125-volt outdoor receptacles — which is the outlet your transformer plugs into. One important subtlety: GFCI on the transformer's primary side does not extend GFCI protection to the 12V secondary circuit feeding your fixtures.

Section 10

My assessment, in plain English

The situation, simply

You want lights along a garden path. There are maybe six brands worth taking seriously. Four of them make a good light. The differences between the good ones are small.

The difference between where you buy them is not small. It is about four hundred dollars.

Why the priority beats price

Here is the thing worth understanding. These lights sit outdoors. They get watered. They get frozen a little and baked a lot. Something inside them will fail. It always does.

When it fails, one of two things happens. Either you unscrew the top and drop in a new bulb — a few dollars, two minutes. Or you dig the whole thing out of the ground and buy a new one, because the light part is glued inside.

That single difference is worth more over ten years than any price you'll pay today. So I ranked on it first. And then, happily, the fixture that wins on that also happens to be the cheapest one on the table. That doesn't usually happen.

The recommendation

Buy the VOLT six-light brass path kit from Costco. $349.99. About $385 after tax. It ships free.

The headline reason: it is solid brass with a lifetime warranty, sold by a shop that will take it back with no deadline.

Three more, in order:

  1. It is complete. Six lights, the power box, a hundred feet of cable, bulbs, stakes, connectors, even the wire strippers. Nothing else to order.
  2. It is cheaper than its own parts. Six of the same maker's fixtures bought separately cost more than this whole kit. That is not a discount, that is an anomaly in your favour.
  3. You have two ways to get your money back. Costco's guarantee, and VOLT's warranty. Most people get one.

The honest catch: you need a Costco membership, and you can't hold the fixture before you buy it. If neither of those works for you, the recommendation changes.

The runner-up

Buy direct from voltlighting.com. Same metal, same warranty, far more choice of shape and finish, no membership. You'll pay roughly twice as much by the time you've added a transformer and cable, and if something breaks you'll be posting it to Florida. Worth it if you care what the fixtures look like in daylight — and you might.

Who I didn't pick, and why

  • Kichler. A properly good brand. Costs about double, and "brass" is a finish on aluminium rather than solid metal.
  • FX Luminaire. Probably the best-made system in this report. You cannot buy it — trade only. That's all.
  • Philips Hue. Genuinely lovely if you want colour and an app. It's a two-year product at a seven-hundred-dollar price, and the light is glued in.
  • Hampton Bay. A hundred and thirteen dollars for a complete set is a real bargain, and for a rental I'd tell you to buy it. It won't see ten years.
  • Solar. Fifty lumens and two nights of battery. Fine for a corner with no cable. Not for a path you walk.

The uncomfortable part

I could not open a single one of these web pages. Not the Costco listing, not VOLT's warranty, not the tax table. This machine's network blocks all of it. Everything above came from search-engine extracts of those pages — which are usually accurate and occasionally are not.

What that means practically: the $349.99 is very likely right, but you should look at it before you click. So is the 4.8 stars. So is the 10% tax. I have given you every link and every phone number to check them, and I have told you which specific facts I'd check first. I would rather hand you a report that says what it doesn't know than one that sounds certain and isn't.

The second uncomfortable thing: Costco's listing doesn't name which VOLT fixture is in the box. It's brass, it's six, it comes with a transformer. It might not be the shape you'd have chosen.

What I'd actually do, in order

  1. Open the Costco listing. Confirm $349.99, confirm it's still in stock, and read the contents line. Two minutes.
  2. Look at the photo. Decide whether you like the fixture. If you don't, stop and go to voltlighting.com instead.
  3. Check you have an outdoor GFCI outlet within reach of where the transformer will live. If you don't, that's an electrician call and it's the only thing that can blow this budget.
  4. Buy it. Pay by credit card.
  5. Before you dig, lay the cable on the grass and switch it on at dusk. Move the lights around for two evenings. Then bury it. Everyone who skips this step re-digs.
  6. Set the transformer's timer to photocell-on, off at midnight. Cheaper to run, kinder to Mt. Hamilton, and it satisfies the state's control rule for free.
  7. Photograph the wiring, keep the receipt and the box. Both warranties need proof of purchase.

Why I chose it

Because the priority I took from your brief — buy once, keep it serviceable, be able to act today — is satisfied completely by exactly one row of the matrix, and it happens to be the cheapest one. I did not expect that and I checked it twice.

The evidence carrying the most weight, in the order I weighted it:

First, the serviceability rule, weighted heaviest. Solid cast brass with a standard bi-pin socket means the fixture's failure mode is a bulb, not a burial. That single structural fact is worth more to you across a decade than every price difference in this report combined, and it decides four of the six candidates without reference to cost.

Second, the doubled remedy, weighted next. VOLT's written lifetime warranty on brass fixtures, bulbs and transformers is real, specific and names its covered components. Costco's satisfaction guarantee has no expiry date for this product class and a counter six miles from your house. You end up with two independent routes to a remedy — and the single most-repeated complaint about buying VOLT direct, the ship-it-back-first friction, is precisely the thing the Costco channel neutralises. That is not a coincidence I noticed after choosing; it is why I chose.

Third, the price, weighted last and still decisive. $349.99 is $44 below the cost of six of the same maker's cheapest fixtures bought bare, at their sale price, before a transformer exists. When a complete kit undercuts its own parts bill, the burden of proof shifts: the question is no longer "is this good value" but "what's the catch," and after auditing it the catch is a membership card and a fixture shape you don't get to pick.

What I discounted. Brand prestige — FX Luminaire is probably better engineered and it does not matter, because you cannot buy it. Trustpilot's 4.1 — seventy-nine self-selected reviews of a company this size is noise, and I read it for complaint shape only. The BBB null. The unverified resale allegation, which carries zero weight. And Houzz's 5.0, which with no denominator is not a number at all.

What would change my mind

One fact: if the Costco kit turns out to use an integrated LED rather than a replaceable bulb. Costco's listing names "Warm White (2700K) LED GU4 Bulbs" in the contents, and a GU4 bulb listed as a separate line item is a replaceable bulb in a socket — that is the whole basis of the pick. If you open the listing and find the LED is sealed into the fixture, the pick fails its own eliminating rule instantly and my recommendation flips to VOLT direct at voltlighting.com, where the socket type is stated explicitly on every product page. That is the one question worth asking before you pay, and it is question five in the Costco email in §11.

A second, softer trigger: if you tell me you have no Costco membership and don't want one, the arithmetic narrows enough that VOLT direct becomes the sensible pick on convenience alone.
